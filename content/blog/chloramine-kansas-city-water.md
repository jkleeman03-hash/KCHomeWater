If your tap water has a faint pool smell, or your coffee tastes a little off, you're not imagining it. That's almost always **chloramine**, the disinfectant that keeps water safe on its way to your home.

## What chloramine is

Chloramine is chlorine combined with a small amount of **ammonia**. Utilities have used it since the 1930s, and the EPA says more than one in five Americans now drink water treated with it.

Plain chlorine kills germs well, but it fades fast in long pipe networks and reacts with natural matter in river water to form **disinfection byproducts**, some of which are regulated because of long-term health concerns. Chloramine lasts longer in the pipes and forms fewer of those byproducts. That's why so many large utilities have switched.

## Who uses it in Kansas City

Nearly all of them. The City of Liberty lists the major systems in the region that disinfect with monochloramine (the form used in drinking water), including:

- **KC Water** (Kansas City, Missouri)
- **WaterOne** (most of Johnson County)
- **Kansas City Board of Public Utilities** (Wyandotte County)
- **Independence**, **Olathe**, **Lawrence**, **Harrisonville**, **St. Joseph**, and Missouri American Water in **Platte County**

Many smaller cities buy their water from one of these systems, so they get chloramine too. WaterOne says it uses monochloramine specifically to control trihalomethanes, one of the main byproducts of chlorine.

## Is it safe?

Yes, for drinking, cooking, and bathing. The EPA and CDC both consider chloramine levels up to **4 milligrams per liter** safe, and utilities test constantly to stay under that limit.

There are three important exceptions:

- **Fish, turtles, and amphibians.** Chloramine passes straight into their bloodstream through their gills or skin. Aquarium and pond water has to be treated with a dechlorinator labeled for chloramine, sold at any pet store.
- **Kidney dialysis.** Dialysis water has to be completely free of chloramine. Dialysis centers handle this, and anyone doing home dialysis should follow their machine's instructions.
- **Some brewing and fermenting.** Homebrewers and sourdough bakers often find chloramine affects flavor or yeast. That's a matter of taste, not safety.

## Why you can still notice it

Safe doesn't mean unnoticeable. Plenty of people can taste or smell chloramine, especially in:

- Drinking water, coffee, and tea
- Hot showers, where the steam carries the smell
- Ice from the freezer

Some people also feel their skin and hair are drier on chloraminated water, though that's harder to pin down and hard water is often part of it.

## What doesn't remove chloramine

This is where a lot of old advice stops working. Tricks that get rid of chlorine **don't work on chloramine**:

- **Letting water sit out.** Chlorine evaporates overnight. The EPA and CDC both say chloramine doesn't.
- **Boiling.** The EPA says boiling doesn't remove monochloramine either.
- **A water softener.** Softeners remove calcium and magnesium. They don't touch chloramine.
- **A reverse osmosis membrane on its own.** The EPA notes that the RO membrane itself doesn't remove monochloramine. The carbon stages built into an RO system are what handle it.

## What does remove it

**Carbon.** Activated carbon breaks chloramine down as water flows through it. The catch is that chloramine is much slower to break down than chlorine, so the system has to be built for it:

- **Catalytic carbon** is carbon that's been treated so its surface reacts with chloramine faster. It's the standard choice for chloraminated water.
- **Contact time matters.** Water has to spend enough time in the carbon. That's why a properly sized whole-home tank works better than a quick inline cartridge, and why a filter's rated flow has to match your home.
- **Check the certification.** Look for filters certified to **NSF/ANSI 42 for chloramine reduction** specifically. A filter certified only for chlorine, or only for "material safety," hasn't been tested on chloramine.

You have two ways to set this up:

| | Whole-home carbon filter | Carbon at one tap (RO or a faucet filter) |
|---|---|---|
| Where it goes | On the main water line | Under the kitchen sink |
| Covers showers and laundry | Yes | No |
| Covers drinking water | Yes | Yes |
| Best for | Smell in the shower and taste everywhere | Taste in drinking water only |

## What we usually recommend in KC

KC water is chloraminated **and** moderately hard, so most homes do best with a **whole-home carbon filter paired with a softener**. The filter handles taste and smell, and the softener handles scale. If you also want the best possible drinking water, add **reverse osmosis** at the kitchen sink. (Here's how [softeners, filters, and RO compare](/blog/water-softener-vs-filtration-vs-reverse-osmosis).)

One more benefit: taking chloramine out upstream also protects a softener's resin, which slowly breaks down from constant disinfectant exposure.

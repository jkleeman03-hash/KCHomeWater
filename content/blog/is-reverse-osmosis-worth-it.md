Most people who ask about reverse osmosis already have a fridge filter or a pitcher, or they're hauling cases of bottled water home every week. So the real question is whether RO is enough better to be worth it.

## What a reverse osmosis system is

An under-sink RO system pushes your water through a series of stages:

1. **Sediment and carbon filters** catch grit and break down chlorine and chloramine. They also protect the membrane, which disinfectant would slowly damage.
2. **The RO membrane** is a sheet so fine that water molecules pass through but most dissolved solids don't. This is what sets RO apart from a regular filter.
3. **A final carbon or remineralization stage** polishes the taste. Remineralization adds a little calcium and magnesium back so the water tastes crisp instead of flat.

The water comes out of its own small faucet at the sink, and it can also feed your fridge's water and ice.

**Tankless** systems, like the ones we install, make water on demand with a built-in pump instead of storing it in a pressure tank. That frees up space under the sink and gives you steadier flow. Because of the pump, they need an electrical outlet under the sink.

## What each option actually removes

| | Fridge filter | Pitcher filter | Bottled water | Under-sink RO |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chlorine and chloramine taste | Usually | Usually | Yes | Yes |
| Dissolved solids (TDS) | No | Little to none | Varies by brand | Most |
| Lead | If certified | If certified | Varies by brand | Yes, if certified |
| PFAS ("forever chemicals") | Some models | Some models | Varies by brand | Yes, if certified |
| Nitrate and arsenic | No | No | Varies by brand | Yes, if certified |
| Feeds ice maker | Yes | No | No | Yes |

The pattern: fridge and pitcher filters are mostly **carbon**, and carbon is great at taste and smell. Some are certified for lead or PFAS too, but they do almost nothing for dissolved solids. RO handles a much wider range.

### Check the certification, not the marketing

Any filter can say "removes contaminants." Certified ones have been tested:

- **NSF/ANSI 42** covers taste and odor, like chlorine and chloramine.
- **NSF/ANSI 53** covers health-related contaminants, like lead and PFOA/PFOS, for regular filters.
- **NSF/ANSI 58** is the standard for reverse osmosis systems, including tests for PFAS reduction.

Look for the specific contaminant you care about on the certification listing, not just the standard number.

## What it costs over time

Let's say your household drinks and cooks with about **a gallon and a half a day**. That's about **550 gallons a year**.

- **Bottled water.** Whatever you pay per gallon, multiply by 550. Even at a dollar a gallon, that's around $550 a year, plus hauling it home and recycling the bottles.
- **Pitcher filter.** Cheap to buy, but cartridges usually need replacing every few months, and filling and refilling a pitcher gets old fast.
- **Fridge filter.** Easy and convenient, usually replaced about twice a year. Fine for taste, limited beyond that.
- **Under-sink RO.** Higher cost up front for the system and installation. After that, filters are replaced on a schedule, typically every 6 to 12 months depending on the stage, with the membrane lasting longer.

If you're buying bottled water now, RO usually pays for itself within a few years and saves you the trips to the store.

## What about the wastewater?

Reverse osmosis rinses the membrane as it works, so some water goes down the drain. Older tank-style systems were known for sending several gallons down the drain for every gallon of drinking water. Newer tankless systems with built-in pumps are much more efficient. If it matters to you, ask for the system's **efficiency rating** on its performance data sheet, which every NSF/ANSI 58 certified system has.

And because RO only treats drinking and cooking water, the total wastewater is small compared to a shower or a load of laundry.

## Isn't RO water too pure to drink?

You'll see claims that RO water "strips minerals from your body." Most of the minerals you need come from food, not water. That said, very pure water can taste flat. That's why we use systems with a **remineralization stage**, which adds a little calcium and magnesium back for better taste.

## When RO is worth it

RO makes the most sense if:

- You're **buying bottled water** now, or you'd like to stop.
- You want **coffee, tea, and ice** that taste clean.
- You're concerned about **PFAS, lead, nitrate, or arsenic** and want a system certified to reduce them.
- You're on a **private well**, where nobody else is testing your water. (Read more about [well water in the KC area](/blog/well-water-kansas-city).)

You might not need it if your only complaint is a slight chlorine taste. A good whole-home carbon filter fixes that at every tap. (See [why KC water tastes like a pool](/blog/chloramine-kansas-city-water).)

## RO and a softener work together

RO only treats one tap. It won't protect your water heater from scale or fix spotty dishes.

PFAS, or "forever chemicals," have been all over the news. If you live in the Kansas City metro, you've probably wondered whether they're in your tap water. Here's what the testing shows.

## What PFAS are

PFAS (per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances) are a family of thousands of man-made chemicals used since the 1940s in nonstick pans, stain- and water-resistant fabrics, food packaging, and firefighting foam. They're called "forever chemicals" because they break down extremely slowly, in the environment and in the body.

Two of the most studied, **PFOA** and **PFOS**, have been linked to health effects including certain cancers, immune effects, and developmental effects in babies. That's why the EPA set limits on them in drinking water.

## Where the federal rules stand

In April 2024, the EPA set the first national drinking water limits for PFAS:

- **PFOA and PFOS: 4 parts per trillion each.** One part per trillion is about one drop in 20 Olympic swimming pools.
- **PFHxS, PFNA, and HFPO-DA ("GenX"):** 10 parts per trillion each, plus a combined limit for mixtures.

In 2026, the EPA proposed **keeping the PFOA and PFOS limits** but giving water systems until **2031** instead of 2029 to meet them. It also proposed **rescinding** the limits on the other PFAS. As of this writing, those changes are still proposals. Water systems must finish their first round of PFAS monitoring by 2027.

## What testing found in the Kansas City metro

Between 2023 and 2025, the EPA had every large water system in the country test for **29 different PFAS** under its Unregulated Contaminant Monitoring Rule (UCMR 5). We pulled the results for the 19 large systems serving the Kansas City area:

|---|---|
| KC Water (Kansas City, MO) | PFBA, once, at 5.6 ppt |
| WaterOne (Johnson County) | PFBA, twice, up to 5.3 ppt |
| Lee's Summit | PFBA, once, at 5.7 ppt |
| Belton | PFBA, once, at 5.4 ppt |
| Gardner | PFPeA, twice, up to 3.4 ppt |
| Kansas City Board of Public Utilities | None |
| Olathe | None |
| Independence | None |
| Blue Springs | None |
| Raymore | None |
| Raytown | None |
| Liberty | None |
| Gladstone | None |
| Grain Valley | None |
| Leavenworth | None |
| Missouri American Water (Platte County) | None |

**The headline:** none of these systems had **PFOA or PFOS** above the EPA's reporting level of 4 parts per trillion, the same number as the federal limit.

The two PFAS that did show up, **PFBA** and **PFPeA**, are short-chain PFAS. Neither one has a federal drinking water limit, and the levels found were low.

### What these results don't tell you

- **"None" means below the reporting level, not zero.** Each PFAS has a minimum level the labs report, usually 2 to 5 parts per trillion. Smaller amounts may be present but wouldn't show up.
- **This is water leaving the treatment plant.** It doesn't capture what happens in a home's own plumbing.
- **Small systems weren't all tested.** If your water comes from a smaller town or district not on this list, check its annual water quality report or ask the utility.
- **Private wells aren't tested at all** unless you test them. PFAS can show up in groundwater near airports, military bases, industrial sites, and places where firefighting foam was used. If you're on a well and concerned, a state-certified lab can test for PFAS. (More on [well water in the KC area](/blog/well-water-kansas-city).)

KC Water says it began voluntary PFAS testing in 2022 and that all of its results have been below EPA standards. WaterOne has said it hasn't detected PFAS in its system.

## What reduces PFAS at home

If you want an extra margin of safety, three technologies are proven to reduce PFAS:

- **Reverse osmosis.** The most thorough option. An under-sink RO system reduces a wide range of PFAS, including short-chain types that carbon struggles with.
- **Activated carbon.** Traps PFAS as water flows through. It works well on long-chain PFAS like PFOA and PFOS but is less effective on short-chain types like PFBA.
- **Ion exchange resin designed for PFAS.** A specialized resin, mostly used by utilities and in some home systems.

**Things that don't help:** boiling water doesn't remove PFAS, and it can actually concentrate them as water evaporates. A regular water softener isn't designed to remove PFAS either.

### Look for the certification

Penn State Extension recommends looking for systems certified for **"PFOA reduction" and "PFOS reduction"** under:

- **NSF/ANSI 53** for regular filters
- **NSF/ANSI 58** for reverse osmosis systems

NSF/ANSI 58 also includes a **total PFAS reduction** claim. Check the certification listing for the specific claim, not just the standard number.

### Whole house or one tap?

Most PFAS exposure from water comes from drinking it and cooking with it, not from showering. That's why a **point-of-use RO system at the kitchen sink** is the most common and cost-effective choice for PFAS. Whole-house PFAS treatment exists, but it's larger and costs more, and it's usually only worth it when levels are high, like in a contaminated well.

## The bottom line

Based on the EPA's testing, Kansas City's large public water systems aren't showing PFOA or PFOS above the federal limit, and the PFAS that did turn up were found at low levels. That's good news.

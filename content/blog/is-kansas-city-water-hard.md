Short answer: yes. Water across the Kansas City metro is typically **moderately hard to hard**. It's safe to drink, and it meets federal standards, but the minerals in it are hard on your home.

## Where Kansas City water comes from

Kansas City's water comes mostly from the Missouri River. Independence draws from wells along the river, and most of Johnson County is served by WaterOne, which draws from the Missouri and Kansas Rivers. Many surrounding cities buy their water from one of these systems.

As water moves through soil and rock, it picks up dissolved **calcium and magnesium**. Those minerals are what make water "hard." Utilities treat the water to make it safe, but they don't soften it. KC-area utilities also disinfect with **chloramine**, which keeps water safe in the pipes but is behind the chemical taste and smell many people notice.

## Signs you have hard water

- White crust on faucets and showerheads
- Spots on dishes and cloudy glass shower doors
- Soap and shampoo that don't lather well, and soap scum that comes back fast
- Skin that feels tight and hair that looks dull after a shower
- Water heaters, dishwashers, and washing machines that wear out sooner, because scale builds up inside them

## How to check your own water

Your utility publishes an **annual water quality report** (sometimes called a Consumer Confidence Report). Search for your utility's name plus "water quality report" and look for **hardness**. It's usually listed in one of two units:

| Hardness | Milligrams per liter (mg/L) | Grains per gallon (gpg) |
|---|---|---|
| Soft | 0–60 | 0–3.5 |
| Moderately hard | 61–120 | 3.5–7 |
| Hard | 121–180 | 7–10.5 |
| Very hard | Over 180 | Over 10.5 |

To convert, 1 grain per gallon equals about 17.1 mg/L. Ranges follow the U.S. Geological Survey's hardness scale. If the report is confusing, we're happy to help you read it.

## What fixes hard water

A **water softener** is the fix for hardness. It captures calcium and magnesium on resin beads and swaps them for sodium, so every tap, shower, and appliance gets soft water. The resin cleans itself on a schedule, and all you do is keep the salt topped off.

A softener doesn't remove chloramine or improve taste. That's what **whole-home carbon filtration** does, and the two are often installed together. For the purest drinking water at the kitchen sink, many homeowners add **reverse osmosis**.

A water softener is a tank on your main water line that pulls the **calcium and magnesium** out of your water before it reaches any faucet, shower, or appliance. Those two minerals are what make water "hard," and they're what leave white crust on fixtures and scale inside your water heater.

That's the whole job: it fixes hardness. It doesn't filter out chlorine or chloramine, and it doesn't change how your water tastes. (For that, see [softener vs. filtration vs. reverse osmosis](/blog/water-softener-vs-filtration-vs-reverse-osmosis).)

Here's what's inside one, how it works, how to size it, and what the brand name on the tank does and doesn't change.

## What a water softener is made of

Almost every residential softener, from the cheapest to the most expensive, uses the same handful of parts.

**Mineral tank (resin tank).** The tall tank that does the softening. Most are a polyethylene liner wrapped in fiberglass, which is why you'll see them called **FRP tanks** (fiberglass-reinforced plastic). They're sized by diameter and height in inches, so a "10x54" tank is 10 inches wide and 54 inches tall.

**Resin.** The tank is filled with thousands of tiny plastic beads, each smaller than a BB. The beads are polystyrene with a negative charge built into them, and that charge is what grabs hardness minerals. Resin is measured in cubic feet, and the amount of resin is what sets a softener's capacity.

**Control valve (the "head").** The valve on top of the mineral tank is the brains of the system. It routes water through the resin during normal use and runs the cleaning cycle when the resin is full. It also decides *when* to clean: older valves run on a timer, and most modern ones are **metered**, meaning they count the gallons you use and only regenerate when needed.

**Distributor tube and baskets.** A pipe runs down the middle of the tank with a slotted basket at the bottom (and often one at the top). Water spreads out through the resin instead of tunneling straight through, and the baskets keep the beads in the tank. Some tanks also have a layer of gravel at the bottom to help spread the flow.

**Brine tank.** The shorter tank next to it holds the salt. Water sits in the bottom and dissolves the salt into **brine**, a very salty water used to clean the resin. A **brine well** inside holds the float, and a **safety float** shuts off the refill so the tank can't overflow.

**Bypass valve.** A set of handles that lets you route water around the softener for repairs, or for outdoor faucets if they're on the same line.

## How a water softener works

### Softening: ion exchange

Calcium and magnesium are positively charged once they're dissolved in water. The resin beads are coated with sodium, which is also positively charged but held more loosely. As water flows down through the tank, the beads grab the calcium and magnesium and let go of sodium in their place. That trade is called **ion exchange**.

The water that comes out the bottom is soft. It now carries a small amount of sodium instead of the hardness minerals.

### Regeneration: cleaning the resin

Eventually the beads are covered in calcium and magnesium and can't hold any more. The control valve then runs a **regeneration** cycle, usually at night when nobody's using water:

1. **Backwash.** Water runs up through the tank in reverse, loosening the resin bed and flushing out sediment.
2. **Brine draw.** Brine is pulled from the salt tank into the resin. With that much sodium around, the beads release the calcium and magnesium and take sodium back on.
3. **Rinse.** Fresh water rinses the leftover brine and the hardness minerals down the drain.
4. **Refill.** The valve adds water back to the brine tank so it can dissolve more salt for next time.

The whole cycle takes around one to two hours. On a metered valve, it only happens when you've actually used the softener's capacity, which saves salt and water compared to a timer.

### What you do

Keep salt in the brine tank. Most homes add a bag or two every month or two. If you'd rather avoid the added sodium, **potassium chloride** works in place of salt, though it costs more.

## What size water softener does a home need?

Softeners are sold by **grain capacity**: how many grains of hardness they can remove before they need to regenerate. Sizing comes down to two questions.

### 1. How much hardness does your household use?

The standard rule of thumb:

> **People in the home × 75 gallons per person per day × your water hardness (grains per gallon) = grains per day**

You want a softener that can handle about a week of that before regenerating. Too small and it regenerates every day or two, which wastes salt and water. Much too large and you're paying for capacity you'll never use.

Here's how that works out for water at **10 grains per gallon**, a common level for moderately hard to hard water:

| Household | Grains per day | Grains per week | Typical resin | Typical label |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1–2 people | 1,500 | 10,500 | 1 cu ft | "32,000 grain" |
| 3–4 people | 3,000 | 21,000 | 1.5 cu ft | "48,000 grain" |
| 5–6 people | 4,500 | 31,500 | 2 cu ft | "64,000 grain" |
| 7 or more | 5,250+ | 36,750+ | 2.5 cu ft or larger | "80,000 grain" |

Your own hardness number is in your utility's annual water quality report. If it's listed in mg/L, divide by 17.1 to get grains per gallon. (Our post on [Kansas City water hardness](/blog/is-kansas-city-water-hard) walks through it.) Well water with iron needs extra capacity, because iron uses up resin too.

**Why the labels look bigger than you need:** the grain number on the box assumes the softener uses the maximum amount of salt every time it regenerates. Most installers set the salt lower, because it's much more efficient, and at that setting real capacity is closer to two-thirds of the label. That's why a household needing 21,000 grains a week ends up with a "48,000 grain" unit.

### 2. How much water does your home use at once?

Capacity is about how much water you use in a day. **Flow rate** is about how much you use in a minute, with the shower, dishwasher, and a sink all running together. A softener that's too small for your home's peak flow will cause a drop in water pressure.

Bigger resin tanks handle more flow, and so does a larger valve (1 inch instead of ¾ inch). That's why bathroom count matters as much as household size. A couple in a four-bathroom house may need a bigger softener than the weekly math alone suggests.

## Name brand vs. wholesale: what's actually different?

This is where shopping for a softener gets confusing. Here's how the main options break down.

### Culligan

Culligan is the best-known name in water treatment. Its dealer-installed softeners are built around **Culligan's own control valves**, and they're sold and serviced through a network of local Culligan dealers, often with rental or lease options. You get a single company to call for service. The trade-offs are price, and that repairs and parts generally go back through the Culligan dealer.

### Kinetico

Kinetico takes a genuinely different approach. Its softeners are **non-electric**: no power cord, timer, or circuit board. Most use **two resin tanks**, so one softens while the other regenerates, and the flow of water itself drives the valve and decides when to regenerate. Kinetico sells through **authorized independent dealers**. These are well-built systems, and they're priced at the high end of the market.

### Pentair

Pentair is a little different from the other two, and it's the key to understanding "wholesale" systems. Pentair sells its own branded softeners through dealers, but it also owns **Fleck**, which makes some of the most widely used softener control valves in the world, and **Structural**, a major maker of FRP tanks. Pentair bought Fleck in 1995, and the Fleck 5600 valve has been in production for over 40 years.

So when you see a softener with no famous name on it, there's a good chance the valve and tank came from Pentair anyway.

### Wholesale and independent systems

A "wholesale" or non-name-brand softener is usually assembled from **industry-standard parts**: a Fleck or Clack control valve, an FRP tank, high-capacity cation resin, and a standard brine tank. These are the same kinds of components many dealers use, just without a brand name on the cabinet and without a dealer network's overhead built into the price.

What that gets you:

- **Lower cost** for the same capacity.
- **Parts you can get anywhere.** Fleck and Clack valves, standard tanks, and resin are sold by many suppliers, and any water treatment pro or plumber who knows softeners can service them.
- **Easy to rebuild.** When the resin wears out years from now, you can replace the resin instead of the whole system.

What to watch for:

- **Installation quality matters more.** A dealer brand comes with the dealer's installer. With a wholesale system, it's only as good as whoever sizes, programs, and installs it.
- **Warranty comes from different places.** The tank, valve, and resin may each carry their own manufacturer warranty, and labor is covered by the installer, not a national brand. Ask who you'd call if something goes wrong.

### Side by side

| | Culligan / Kinetico (dealer brands) | Wholesale (standard components) |
|---|---|---|
| How it softens | Ion exchange with resin and salt | Ion exchange with resin and salt |
| Control valve | Brand's own design | Fleck, Clack, or similar |
| Where you buy it | Local authorized dealer | Independent installer or supplier |
| Parts and service | Usually through the dealer | Widely available from many sources |
| Price | Higher | Lower |
| Biggest variable | Which dealer you work with | Who sizes and installs it |

### What about big-box store softeners?

The all-in-one cabinet softeners sold at home improvement stores are a third category. They're the cheapest up front and fine for some homes, but they often use proprietary parts, smaller resin beds, and lighter-duty valves, and they're usually installed by the homeowner. They're generally not built to the same standard as either a dealer brand or a system made from commercial-grade components.

## The bottom line

Every softener on this list removes hardness the same way: resin beads trading sodium for calcium and magnesium, cleaned with brine from a salt tank. The differences come down to the valve, how the system is sold and serviced, and price.

The softeners we install are built on **Fleck control valves**, the same valves Pentair has been making for decades, so any water treatment pro can service them and parts are easy to find for years to come.

When you compare quotes, ask:

1. **How much resin is in the tank?** Compare cubic feet, not the grain number on the label.
2. **What control valve does it use**, and can someone besides the seller service it?
3. **Is it metered** so it only regenerates when you've used the water?
4. **How did you size it?** A good answer mentions your hardness, your household, and your bathrooms.
5. **What does the warranty cover**, for how long, and who handles labor?

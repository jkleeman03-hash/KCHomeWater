A water softener is one of the lowest-maintenance things in your house. But it does need one thing from you: **salt**. Here's which kind to buy, how much you'll use, and the two problems worth knowing about.

## Why a softener needs salt

The resin beads inside a softener grab calcium and magnesium from your water and give up sodium in exchange. Every so often the resin fills up, and the softener rinses it with **brine**, very salty water made in the salt tank, to recharge it. (Here's the full explanation of [how a water softener works](/blog/what-is-a-water-softener).)

No salt means no brine, and no brine means hard water comes right through. The softener won't warn you. Your water just slowly turns hard again.

## Which salt to buy

You'll see a few kinds at the hardware store.

| Type | What it is | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Evaporated pellets** | Refined salt pressed into pellets | Purest, least residue, least likely to clump | Costs a bit more |
| **Solar crystals** | Sea water dried in the sun | Good value, works well | A little more residue than pellets |
| **Rock salt** | Mined salt, least processed | Cheapest | Most dirt and insoluble matter, needs regular tank cleanouts |
| **Potassium chloride** | A salt substitute | No added sodium | Costs several times more, may need a higher setting |

**Our recommendation:** evaporated pellets or solar crystals. Rock salt works, but Cargill, one of the biggest salt makers, recommends it only if you're willing to clean out the brine tank regularly.

**Avoid "rust remover" or "iron fighter" salt unless you need it.** It's meant for well water with iron. On KC city water, regular salt is all you need.

### What about sodium?

A softener adds a small amount of sodium to your water: roughly **8 milligrams per liter for every grain per gallon of hardness** it removes. For a typical KC home, that's a modest amount compared to most foods, but if you're on a sodium-restricted diet, talk to your doctor. You can switch to **potassium chloride**, or drink from a [reverse osmosis system](/blog/is-reverse-osmosis-worth-it), which removes most of the sodium.

## How much salt you'll use

Most homes add **a 40-pound bag or two every month or two**. How much you actually use depends on:

- **How hard your water is.** Harder water fills the resin faster.
- **How many people live there.** More water used means more regenerations.
- **How the softener is set.** A **metered** valve only regenerates when you've used the capacity, which saves a lot of salt compared to an old timer-based softener.

## How full to keep the tank

Check the salt about **once a month**. Keep the tank **about a third to two-thirds full**, and keep the salt above the water level. Overfilling isn't helpful. Packing the tank to the top in a humid basement makes clumping more likely.

## Two problems to watch for

### Salt bridges

A **salt bridge** is a hard crust of salt that forms above the water in the tank. The tank looks full, but there's an empty gap underneath. The salt never touches the water, so no brine gets made, and your water slowly turns hard again.

**How to spot it:** the tank looks full but the salt level hasn't dropped in weeks, and you're starting to see spots on dishes or white crust on fixtures again.

**How to fix it:** gently push a broom handle down through the salt to break the crust. Don't hit the tank walls hard, since you could crack them. Then run a manual regeneration so the resin recharges.

Humidity, temperature swings, and overfilling all make bridges more likely.

### Mushing

**Mushing** is when salt breaks down into a sludge at the bottom of the tank. It can block the brine intake so the softener can't draw brine.

**How to fix it:** Cargill recommends removing the good salt, scooping out the mush, and reloading the good salt. This happens more with lower-grade salt.

## Other upkeep

- **Brine tank cleanout.** Every year or two, or more often with rock salt, empty and rinse the brine tank to clear out residue.
- **Resin.** The resin beads usually last **10 years or more**. Chlorine and chloramine slowly wear them down, which is one more reason a carbon filter ahead of the softener helps. When the resin wears out, you can replace just the resin, not the whole system.
- **Power outages.** Most modern valves keep their settings, but check that the clock is right after a long outage so regenerations still happen at night.
- **Vacation.** Nothing to do. A metered softener simply won't regenerate while nobody's using water.

## Signs something's wrong

Call for service if:

- Hard water symptoms come back and the salt isn't bridged
- The salt level never drops
- The brine tank is full of water up near the top
- You hear the softener running during the day, or it never seems to regenerate
